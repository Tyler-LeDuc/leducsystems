Scan to email for a small office with no IT person

If you run a small office, the copier scans to email, and nobody on staff is "the IT person", this is for you. Microsoft is changing how copiers sign in to Microsoft 365 at the end of December 2026, and many copiers will stop sending scans unless someone changes a setting. Here is how I'd handle it, one step at a time, with no jargon you don't need.

What is changing, in one paragraph

Most office copiers send scans by signing in to Microsoft 365 with an email address and password. Microsoft calls that SMTP AUTH with basic authentication. Microsoft has announced that for existing organizations it is disabled by default at the end of December 2026. An admin can turn it back on for a while, and Microsoft says it will announce the final removal date in the second half of 2027. If your copier works this way and nobody does anything, expect scans to stop arriving in early 2027. (Microsoft's announcement.)

Step 1: are you on Microsoft 365 at all?

If your work email is Outlook with your own domain, and you pay Microsoft (or a reseller like GoDaddy) for it, you probably are. To be sure, type your domain into the domain checker. If you're on Gmail or another provider, this change doesn't apply to you.

Step 2: look at the copier's email settings

On a computer in the office, type the copier's IP address into a browser (it's usually printed on a network settings page from the copier's menu), and sign in as administrator. The admin password is often in the manual or on a sticker; your copier dealer will know. Find the email or SMTP settings and write down three things: the server, the port, and whether there's a username and password filled in. Where the settings are, by brand.

If the server is smtp.office365.com with a username and password, your copier is affected. If it's something ending in mail.protection.outlook.com with no password, it isn't. Not sure? answer three quick questions and you'll get a straight answer.

Step 3: find out who looks after your Microsoft 365

Someone has an admin sign-in for your Microsoft 365. In a small office it's often the owner, an office manager, whoever set up email years ago, or the company you bought it from. Find out who before December, because every route below needs an admin at least once.

While you're there, ask them one question: are "security defaults" turned on? (They're in the Microsoft Entra admin center, under Entra ID, Overview, Properties.) Security defaults make everyone use multi-factor sign-in and block password-only sign-ins like the copier's. Microsoft notes that with security defaults on, SMTP AUTH is already off. If they're on and the copier still scans, it most likely isn't signing in to smtp.office365.com with a password. Either way, don't turn security defaults off just for the copier; it weakens sign-in for everyone.

Step 4: pick the simplest route that fits

Answer two questions:

  1. Can your copier sign in with OAuth (often shown as "Microsoft 365", "OAuth 2.0" or "modern authentication" in the email settings, sometimes only after a firmware update)? Call your copier dealer and ask, with the exact model. If yes, that's the cleanest answer, and the dealer can usually set it up on a service visit.
  2. Does anyone scan to people outside the office, like clients, an accountant or a personal Gmail? Ask the staff, because someone usually does.

Then:

  • Copier has OAuth: have the dealer switch it over. Done.
  • No OAuth, scans only go to your own staff: Direct Send (no password, port 25 to your domain's mail.protection.outlook.com address) can work, but outgoing port 25 must be open on your internet connection, and messages can land in junk unless your SPF record lists the office's public IP. Microsoft's High Volume Email is another internal-only route, with an Azure billing setup.
  • No OAuth, and scans go outside the office: this is the common case. A connector needs a static public IP, which most small offices don't have. A relay on an office PC doesn't. That's what Scan Relay, which I make, does: it runs on a Windows PC that stays on, the copier sends to that PC instead of smtp.office365.com, and it delivers through Microsoft 365 with modern sign-in, to anyone inside or outside. It works with security defaults on. The catch is that the PC has to stay on, and scans over about 3 MB need splitting on the copier.
  • Out of time: an admin can turn SMTP AUTH back on for the copier's mailbox, for now. It's a stopgap. How, and what it costs you.

Step 5: test before December, not after

Whatever you pick, set it up while the old way still works. Then if a test scan doesn't arrive, you change the copier's settings back and try again next week, instead of having no scanning in January. Test three scans: one to yourself, one to a coworker, and one to an outside address if anyone scans outside the office. Check the junk folder too.

Step 6: write it down

Tape a note inside the copier cabinet or keep it with your office records: which route you chose, the copier's new server and port, which mailbox scans come from, and who to call. The next person to touch it, maybe you in two years, will thank you.

Questions

Do I need to buy a new copier?

Usually not. Even a copier with no OAuth can keep scanning to email through Direct Send, a connector or a relay on an office PC, depending on who it sends to.

Our copier dealer handles everything. Do I need to do anything?

Ask them, before December, whether they've checked your scan to email for the Microsoft 365 change. Dealers often look after the copier but not your Microsoft 365, and the fix sometimes needs both.

Can I just change the password on the copier?

No. The password isn't the problem; Microsoft is turning off this way of signing in, whatever the password.

Where does Microsoft explain the settings?

Enable or disable SMTP AUTH in Exchange Online, Security defaults in Microsoft Entra ID, and How to set up a multifunction device or application to send email. Microsoft updates these and has moved dates before, so check them for the current details.
